## Project Inception and Reasoning
The Context-Aware Assistive Navigational Tool (ANT) was conceived to address the "one-size-fits-all" limitations of current navigation apps, which consistently fail to accommodate the diverse accessibility needs of disabled individuals. Existing mobile solutions often lack cognitive support, proactive hazard detection, and contextual safety intelligence (such as real-time crime or weather data). This gap creates a daily struggle with unpredictable hazards, transit confusion, neighborhood safety concerns, and high cognitive load, ultimately leading to increased risk and isolation during urban commutes.

## The Core Objective
ANT is a mobile application that acts as a digital "local expert," providing real-time, personalized navigational support directly from the user's smartphone. It moves beyond simple A-to-B routing by integrating proactive advance warnings for hazards, personalized transit assurance (such as confirming the correct bus), and actionable contextual reminders to prevent accidents and physical fatigue. The primary goal is to restore independence, lower the psychological cognitive load, and ensure safe, equitable urban mobility.

## Target Demographics and Adaptive Support
The app dynamically adapts its on-screen interface and routing logic to distinct user profiles through specific accessibility filters:
* **Mobility Impaired:** The mobile routing algorithm is heavily optimized for zero-curbs, ramps, and elevator-equipped transit stations.
* **Visually Impaired:** The app prioritizes the smartphone's native haptic vibration motor and text-to-speech audio for orientation and hazard alerts, utilizing the phone's built-in camera for environmental scanning.
* **Hearing Impaired / Deaf:** The interface must be highly visual, simple, and easy to interact with on-screen. The LLM assistant provides real-time transcriptions and dynamic visual text guidance in place of audio.
* **Cognitive Impairment:** The mobile interface reduces cognitive load via highly simplified on-screen visual cues, repetitive audio confirmations, and automated "Safe Person" text alerts to trusted contacts.
* **High Anxiety/PTSD:** The app actively reroutes users away from high-crime historical hotspots or highly crowded areas to ensure emotional comfort and personal security.

## Mobile-Centric System Architecture
To build the ANT application, the architecture relies exclusively on the smartphone's built-in hardware, edge processing, and cloud APIs:
* **Sensing Layer (Data In):** The system ingests raw data using the smartphone's built-in camera for on-demand snapshot capture, the phone's GPS/gyroscopes for location and movement, and API calls to Open-weather, Google Maps, and Crime Statistics databases for environmental context.
* **Perception & Analysis Layer:** The app utilizes Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) processing the phone's camera feed to identify physical hazards (obstacles, uneven terrain) and perform object detection (like identifying specific bus numbers from signs).
* **Control & Output Layer:** A decision engine calculates a real-time route risk score using a weighted algorithm: $R=w_1(\text{Crime Density}) + w_2(\text{Terrain Difficulty}) + w_3(\text{Weather Condition})$.
* **Multimodal Feedback:** Natural Language Processing (NLP) generates simplified, step-by-step audio instructions through the phone's speaker, while translating directions and hazard warnings into specific haptic vibration patterns using the device's native vibration motor.

## Chapter 3. Research Results and Discussions
16  people  participated;  primary  data  collection  involved  co-design  sessions  and  in-depth  interviews,  our  main  method.   This  covered  two  special  sessions:  a  follow-up  session  with  10 participants with various physical problems, such as deafness, incapacity to walk, and cognitive difficulties, and one with six people who were partially or completely blind.  While we focused a lot on qualitative data collection, the following results are a reflection of the user’s lived experiences, daily struggles, and emotional realities.  We found five major thematic findings.  However, five key design objectives for our next assistive navigation application are directly affected by the findings.

### 3.1  Thematic Findings
#### 3.1.1  Thematic Finding 1: Navigation and Spatial Disorientation
The  main  problem  with  navigation  was  not  reaching  the  destination.   It  was  losing  track  of their  current  location  in  the  middle  of  the  journey.   The  majority  of  the  participants  used  a white cane to read the road, leading to their own findings.  This was successful until something changed.  The system may completely break up at an under-construction site or an unfamiliar turn.  Participants said that they needed a constant assurance of where they are throughout, not just a route from point A to point B. A specific and recurring problem was missing a bus stop without realising it and then not knowing where they had ended up.  One participant put it plainly:  ”Sometimes when the bus goes past our destination, and we’ve gone far, we don’t even realise it,  and then we end up in a much bigger problem.” (P6,  Session 1) ”Location-related information” was coded 34 times across 5 documents.  ”Inform pathway and directional signal” appeared 33 times across 6 documents.  ”Destination” was coded 26 times across 5 documents.

#### 3.1.2  Thematic Finding 2: Emotional Strain and Travel Anxiety
Emotional  difficulty  came  up  across  all  15  participants  and  across  all  sessions.   It  was  not described as occasional.  Participants talked about anxiety before leaving the house, frustration during a journey, and longer-term exhaustion from managing these challenges every day.  Several described anticipatory stress:  the feeling of already being worn out before a trip had started. Not  getting  ready  in  time,  depending  on  others,  falling  behind  –  these  specific  frustrations accumulated.  For some participants, they had built into something closer to depression.  ”I just cry.  I am very emotional.  I just cry.” (P1/P2, Session 1) One finding that directly shapes design is that participants were explicit about how they wanted to be spoken to.  ”Mental pressure” was the most frequently coded tag across the dataset, appearing 49 times.  ”Fear” was coded 25 times across 6 documents.  ”Worry about punctuality” appeared 9 times.

#### 3.1.3  Thematic Finding 3: Physical Safety and Environmental Hazards
Participants described Dhaka’s streets as routinely dangerous and named specific hazards they dealt with regularly.  Open manholes were raised in multiple sessions.  Broken pavements were also a consistent concern.  When it rained, participants described being unable to reach a safe place.  Road crossings were another source of fear; one participant described wishing Bangladesh had  signals  that  stop  vehicles  before  pedestrians  cross,  the  way  some  countries  do.   Crime was  another  dimension:   one  participant  described  actively  avoiding  certain  roads  based  on their  history  of  mugging  and  theft.   Across  all  of  these  hazards,  the  critical  finding  was  the same:  no one had any warning system.  With no digital tool alerting them to what was about to  happen,  they  were  travelling  reactively  and  in  real  time.   “Danger  does  not  come  with  a warning.   However,  I  need  technology  that  alerts  me  in  advance  so  I  may  take  precautions before it occurs.” (P1/P2, Session 1) In three documents, ”fear for physical safety” was coded 22 times.  ”Environment” was used 19 times in five different documents.  In three documents, ”Warning”  was  coded  thirteen  times.   ”Crossing  the  road”  was  used  twelve  times  in  three different documents.

#### 3.1.4  Thematic Finding 4: Social Dependence and Loss of Dignity
For participants, asking strangers for assistance was the main strategy that enabled journeys. They frequently relied on bus drivers, store owners, and bystanders for directions, stop confirmation, and road crossings.  Sometimes the assistance was available right away, and other times it was not at all.  A number of individuals reported feeling hurt when they were disregarded or mistreated, as well as reluctant to ask for assistance.  One participant developed a deliberate workaround:  instead of asking random bystanders, they would intentionally search for a nearby shopkeeper, assuming that shopkeepers are more familiar with the region than those who are merely passing through.
”I  don’t  usually  ask  random  people  on  the  street.   I  look  for  a  nearby  shop  or  stall,  because shopkeepers usually know the area well and can show me the right way.” (P5,  Session 1) Participants did not want social interaction to be eliminated from their commutes, despite the challenges.  According to the data,  people clearly prefer technology that facilitates these connections and makes them safer to act upon rather than technology that replaces them.  ”Social interaction” was coded 34 times across 5 documents.  ”Reliance on passerby” was coded 31 times across 5 documents.  ”Help” appeared 22 times across 5 documents.  ”Feeling hesitant” was coded 5 times.

#### 3.1.5  Thematic Finding 5: Public Transit Confusion and Exclusion
Buses and rickshaws were the primary modes of transportation for the majority of participants. Both were consistently challenging to use.  Without sight, it was mostly a matter of guesswork to  identify  the  correct  bus  because  numbers  and  destination  boards  are  visual.   Since  most Dhaka city buses lack audio stop announcements, participants reported asking the conductor or  driver  for  assistance  when  they  were  not  sure  if  they  had  arrived  at  their  destination.   It was mentioned that calling a rickshaw was a daily struggle.  Participants reported encountering drivers  who  refused  to  stop  for  them.   ”The  vehicles  don’t  stop.   The  drivers  don’t  want  to take me.” (Session 2) One participant said that they were completely unable to board public buses.  Another talked about how hard it is to get into any car and then find a seat that is not occupied.  These were not insignificant inconveniences; rather, they severely restricted the participants’ freedom of movement.
”Usage of public transport” was coded 10 times across 3 documents.  ”Transportation problem” appeared 6 times across 4 documents.  ”Rickshaw usage” was coded 4 times.  ”Get off” and ”Bus stand” each appeared 4 times.

### 3.2  Design Goals
#### 3.2.1  Design Goal 1: Continuous Spatial Orientation
The navigation results in section 3.1.1 demonstrated that maintaining users’ orientation throughout the trip, rather than simply at the beginning and end, is the largest gap.  The app should offer continuous, real-time audio assistance based on the user’s precise GPS position, not just at  decision  points.   When  a  user  pauses,  it  should  validate  position,  describe  the  immediate surroundings, and automatically reorient when the user veers off course.  Compared to a turn-by-turn GPS, this is more similar to a narrating companion.

#### 3.2.2  Design Goal 2: Low Anxiety, Calm Interaction Design
The findings in section 3.1.2 showed that anxiety is built into the travel experience from the start.   An  app  that  increases  cognitive  burden  while  resolving  logistical  and  physical  issues won’t be helpful.  Every component of the app’s interaction model should be made with stress reduction in mind.  The audio output should be slow and serene.  Instructions must be brief and clear.  Regular confirmation cues should ensure that the user knows they are still headed in the right direction.  Notifications should be necessary rather than frequent.  Given that participants’ preferences for information volume varied, the app should also let users modify the amount of feedback they receive. Participants explicitly requested polite, human-like communication; thus, the tone of the voice output is crucial.

#### 3.2.3  Design Goal 3: Proactive Hazard Alerts
Participants were navigating a hazardous environment without an advanced warning system, according to the findings in section 3.1.4.  The design response must work in tandem with the user.  The app should incorporate data on construction, weather, road conditions, and hazards identified by the community.  It should also notify users before they approach a dangerous area. Haptic feedback should be used as a secondary channel after voice alerts, eliminating the need for  screen  interaction.   In  an  emergency,  a  one-touch  option  should  provide  direction  to  the closest safe spot and communicate the user’s location with a reliable contact.

#### 3.2.4  Design Goal 4: Safe Social Interaction Support
The findings in section 3.1.4 disclose that interaction with passersby is a fundamental navigation technique with both practical and emotional results.  The idea is not to replace these existing relationships but to make them safer and more respectful.  A ”show screen” feature would allow users to display a clear written request on screen for a passerby to see, decreasing friction in a loud environment and providing users with a mechanism to confidently start the interactions.  A basic GPS-based way to check could flag if a stranger’s guidance is inconsistent with the planned path.  Location sharing with trusted contacts should be simple to activate and deactivate, giving users a safety net without removing their freedom.

#### 3.2.5  Design Goal 5: Informal Transit Integration
As  highlighted  in  Section  3.1.5,  Dhaka’s  bus  and  rickshaw  system  is  too  unpredictable  and informal for accurate navigation instructions to be useful.  The app needs to be built for this specific circumstance.  This shows that passengers are given a fast choice in a situation where their  stop  is  missed,  are  alerted  beforehand  when  their  stop  is  coming  by  GPS  instead  of announcements, and are helped to locate the correct bus using audio and contextual clues.  For rickshaw estimates, adding a display element that shows the location and cost might streamline negotiation.  Since Dhaka’s transportation data is not reliably available from any single source, the program should be built to include crowdsourced and community-contributed data from the beginning.

## Chapter 4. Project Design & Requirements

### 4.1. Requirement Analysis

#### Functional Requirements
1. Bangla AI Assistant & Map Navigation: The system must include a map layout, which the AI uses to guide the user through their journeys. The system must function fully through conversational voice commands and dialogue using an AI assistant that speaks Bangla. By default, the AI is always on, but users have the option to turn it off. This includes an initial setup stage upon first opening the app, where the user (independently or with assistance from an able-bodied person) can configure their specific disability types, preferences, and daily lifestyle requirements either through voice dialogue with the AI or manually via the standard app interface.
2. Multi-Way Communication: The system communicates to the user via audio(voice), vibrations (haptics), or displays simple pictures/text dynamically.
3. Personalized Settings: Users can set their specific disability in their personal profile so the app may offer them proper advice.
4. Safety Alerts: The application will warn users about dangers like high-crime areas(recorded from historical data) , broken roads/holes , or weather changes like sudden heavy rain.
5. Bus & Vehicle Finder: The system must use the phone’s camera to ”see” which bus is coming and tell the user if it is the right one.
6. The Magic Button: A special button (or voice command) that will instantly call the family, police or hospitals nearby and send them the user’s exact location.
7. Get Back on Track: If a user takes a wrong turn and gets confused, the system must immediately give simple steps like voice commands/haptic alarms/visual notifications and directions to get back to a safe path.
8. Safe-Route First: The system should focus on the safest road instead of picking the nearest routes even if it takes a few minutes longer than the shortest road.
9. Outdoor Help: The system must work reliably on the street. (Note: Indoor navigation in confusing places like train stations or malls is heavily limited by smartphone GPS capabilities, so the primary scope remains outdoor assistance).
10. Virtual Guardian: A family member can watch the trip/location(live) on their own phone and get an alert only if the user stops moving or enters a dangerous area or presses the magic button.
11. Deaf Accessibility: The system must provide a fully visual and text-based interface alternative to the voice assistant for deaf users, ensuring the LLM can assist through simplified on-screen interactions.

#### Non-Functional Requirements
1. Easy to Understand & Calming UI: The system must feature a highly simplified, uncluttered visual interface. The design should use calming and soothing colors and fonts to reduce cognitive load and prevent anxiety.
2. Super Fast Alerts: If there is a danger (broken roads/hole in the road), then the system must warn and send dynamic alerts to the user in less than half a second.
3. Works Offline: If the device loses internet connection, the app gracefully degrades. GPS tracking continues, pre-cached map tiles display, the local TFLite hazard detection works to prevent immediate danger, and the offline STT handles basic emergency commands. The AI calmly notifies the user: 'Internet is unavailable. Basic safety mode is active.'
4. Calm Voice: The AI assistant should use a very calm and friendly voice to help the user be relaxed in crowded or noisy places and not make them feel more anxiety.
5. Privacy: The user’s location and camera data must be kept secret and only shared with people the user trusts(like family, police stations, hospitals etc.).
6. Self-Sustaining Automation: After the initial setup stage, the app must be completely self-functioning. Users should not need to manually trigger features, navigate complex menus, or change settings during their journey.
